Data

The dataset contains 1462 survey responses about four cities — Dubai, Rio de Janeiro, New York City, and Paris — collected from ten questions (Q1–Q10) and response id. Each response is labelled with the city it was actually describing (Label), which is the target we predict.

01Missing values

Before any exploration, we checked for missing data. Q10 was missing in 43 of 1462 rows (2.94%). Six other columns (Q2, Q3, Q5, Q7, Q8, Q9) were each missing exactly one value, affecting four distinct rows total.

Decision: the four rows missing a single-value field were dropped — losing under 0.3% of the data was preferable to imputing across six unrelated questions in the same rows. Missing Q10 was filled with an empty string rather than dropped.

02Feature distributions

Q1–Q4 are ratings for popularity, virality, architectural uniqueness, street-party association; Q7–Q9 are numeric questions (temperature guess, height guess, distance guess); Q5 is a select list of travel companions.

Q1–Q4 are all left-skewed with higher ratings — nearly no responses rate a city below 3, and Q1 (popularity) is very high, with over half the responses at 5. Q7– Q9 was very noisy: many values overwhelmingly stayed around zero, but the distribution has a long tail stretching to 10,000 (Q7) and 800–1000 (Q8, Q9) — these outliers will hide the real shape of the distribution near zero, which is a problem we address later.
Reading it: "Partner,Friends" and "Friends" are the two most common answers, as well as "Partner" and "Partner,Friends,Siblings,Co-worker" make up majority of the responses. . Because Q5 is multi-select, Instead of encoding this as seperate 14 categories/features and we interpreted it as 4 indicator features.
Reading it: the four classes are almost perfectly balanced (~365 responses each). This matters directly for the rest of the project since it tells us that accuracy is a fair evaluation metric since there doesn't exist any class imbalance.

03Feature–target relationships

Before deciding which features to keep, we checked (a) whether the numeric features are redundant with each other, and (b) whether each feature actually affects the four cities.

Reading it: Q1, Q2, and Q3 (popularity, virality, architectural uniqueness) are somewhat correlated with each other (r = 0.31–0.45), since a more popular city is likely rated more viral and distinctive too — but not strongly enough to call any of them redundant. Q4 and Q7–Q9 are essentially uncorrelated with everything (|r| < 0.15), meaning these features provides a lot of independent information.
Reading it: Q1–Q4 responses all shift visibly depending on city — Rio has a noticeably lower median for Q1 (popularity) and Q3 (architecture) but the highest Q4 (street-party association, consistent with Carnival), while NYC skews high on Q2 (virality). Q7–Q9, However, show almost no separation by city after the outliers are accounted for. This is because the boxes and whiskers sit on top of each other across all four cities, telling us these three questions themselves give us very little information on the label.
Reading it: companion responses are different meaningfully by city. "Friends" is not a common response for Paris, "Partner,Friends,Siblings,Co-worker" is the most common combination for NYC, and "Partner" is very common toward Paris and NYC over Dubai and Rio. This is why we decomposed Q5 into binary since it's the individual selections, not the original multiple-selection, that have a signal.

04Q6 representation:

Q6 asks respondents to rank six words (Skyscrapers, Sport, Art and Music, Carnival, Cuisine, Economic) from 1–6 by association with the city. We parsed the raw "word=>rank,..." string into six seperate numeric columns, one per word.

Reading it: It is clear that these responses are not uniformly distributed — Skyscrapers is either heavily ranked either 1 or 6, while Cuisine is often ranked in the middle (3–4). This is evidence that word rank correlates with something.
Reading it: There is a clear relation between label and these features shown in these box plots. Dubai and NYC often rank Skyscrapers high while Rio and Paris rank it low; Rio ranks Carnival and Sport far above the other three cities; Paris is the only city ranked high on Cuisine; and Dubai/NYC are ranked high on Economic. These relations justify why each of these words were made into separate columns.

06Q10 representation

Q10 was an open-ended quote about the city. We decided to reduce this feature to two numeric signals: word count, and four keywords (tallest, carnival, eiffel, skyscraper) chosen by inspecting the most frequent words per city.

Reading it: word count is heavily right-skewed (most quotes are 2–8 words) and nearly identical across all four cities — the boxes in the right panel are all almost the same. The four keyword features were rare (15–27 rows each out of 1458) but were veru city-specific when they did appear — e.g. "carnival" only appears in Rio quotes — which is why they were kept despite their sparsity.

08Train / validation / test split

Most respondents answered about more than one city (only 367 unique ids across 1458 remaining rows), so a random row-level split would let the same person's rating appear in both train and test this is a problem because the model could then learn to recognize respondents rather than cities. To address this, we used group-based splitting (based on id) instead, guaranteeing no respondent's rows appear in more than one split.

Class balance still held within every split (24.7%–25.2% per city in each of train/val/test), so the group constraint doesn't introduce any class imbalances. For hyperparameter selection specifically, we additionally ran 5-fold GroupKFold cross-validation within the training set ( still grouped by id), because a single 218-row validation set is noisy enough that its "best" hyperparameter can differ from what 5-fold CV agrees on, which we confirmed while tuning our models. The validation set is then used once, after hyperparameters are fixed, to get an unbiased comparison accuracy number across different model families; the test set remained unseen.
